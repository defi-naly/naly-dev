# Decision Frameworks

## When to Use

- Making a significant resource allocation decision (hiring, investment, product direction)
- Sensing that a decision "feels right" but wanting to pressure-test it
- Choosing between options that all seem reasonable
- Noticing disagreement in a team and wanting structured resolution
- Reviewing a past decision to learn from it

## Core Frameworks

### 1. System 1 vs. System 2 (Kahneman)

Your brain runs two systems. Most bad decisions come from letting System 1 handle what System 2 should own.

| Property | System 1 (Fast) | System 2 (Slow) |
|----------|-----------------|-----------------|
| **Speed** | Instant, automatic | Slow, effortful |
| **Fuel** | Pattern matching, heuristics | Logical reasoning, calculation |
| **Good at** | Familiar situations, quick reads | Novel situations, complex tradeoffs |
| **Fails at** | Statistics, probability, anything new | Sustaining attention, resisting System 1 |
| **Feels like** | Intuition, gut feeling | Thinking hard, concentrating |

**When to trust System 1 (intuition):**
- You have 10,000+ hours of relevant experience in this specific domain
- The environment has regular, predictable patterns (chess, sports, firefighting)
- You are getting clear, immediate feedback on decisions
- The decision is familiar -- you have made similar ones many times

**When to override System 1 and engage System 2:**
- The situation is novel -- you have not faced this exact type of decision before
- The stakes are high and irreversible
- Statistical or probabilistic reasoning is required
- You notice strong emotional pull toward one option
- Multiple people with relevant experience disagree

### 2. The Bias Checklist

Run through these before any major decision. You do not need to eliminate biases -- you need to notice them.

| Bias | What It Does | How to Counter |
|------|-------------|----------------|
| **Anchoring** | First number you hear dominates your estimate | Generate your own estimate before hearing others |
| **Confirmation bias** | You seek evidence that supports what you already believe | Assign someone to argue the opposite case |
| **Availability bias** | Recent or vivid events feel more probable | Ask: "What does the base rate data say?" |
| **Sunk cost fallacy** | You continue because you already invested, not because it is working | Ask: "If I had not already invested, would I start now?" |
| **Planning fallacy** | You underestimate time, cost, and risk for your own projects | Use reference class forecasting (see below) |
| **Survivorship bias** | You study winners and conclude their traits caused success | Actively study failures with similar traits |
| **Narrative fallacy** | You construct a clean story from messy, random events | Ask: "What data would change my mind?" |
| **Overconfidence** | You are more certain than the evidence warrants | Provide confidence intervals, not point estimates |
| **Status quo bias** | You prefer the current state even when change is better | Frame the decision as choosing between two new options |
| **Halo effect** | One positive trait colors your judgment of everything else | Evaluate dimensions independently before aggregating |

### 3. The Pre-Mortem Technique

Before committing to a decision, imagine it has failed spectacularly. Then work backward to figure out why.

**Process:**
1. Gather the decision-making group
2. State the decision clearly
3. Say: "It is one year from now. We went ahead with this decision, and it was a disaster. What went wrong?"
4. Everyone independently writes down 2-3 reasons for failure (3 minutes, silent)
5. Go around the room, each person shares one reason at a time
6. Categorize the failure modes and assess which are most plausible
7. Decide: mitigate the top risks, or change the decision

**Why it works:** It gives people permission to voice concerns that social pressure normally suppresses. In a normal meeting, raising doubts about the plan feels like disloyalty. In a pre-mortem, imagining failure is the assignment.

### 4. Reference Class Forecasting (Kahneman)

The single most powerful debiasing technique for planning. Instead of estimating from the inside (your specific plan, your unique situation), estimate from the outside (how long do projects like this typically take?).

**Process:**
1. Identify the reference class: What category does this project belong to? (product launch, office move, hiring round, feature build)
2. Gather base rates: What is the typical outcome for this category? (How long? What cost? What success rate?)
3. Adjust from the base rate: What makes your situation meaningfully different from the average?
4. Weight heavily toward the base rate: Your "special circumstances" are usually less special than you think.

**Example:**
- Inside view: "Our product launch will take 3 months because we have a clear plan and a strong team."
- Reference class: "Product launches at companies our size typically take 5-8 months."
- Adjusted estimate: "5 months, with a credible range of 4-7 months."

Kahneman found that the inside view is almost always more optimistic than the reference class. The planning fallacy is not occasional -- it is the default state.

### 5. The Scientist Mindset (Grant)

Adam Grant's framework from Think Again: approach your own beliefs with the rigor of a scientist, not the conviction of a preacher, prosecutor, or politician.

| Mode | What You Do | Risk |
|------|-------------|------|
| **Preacher** | Defend your existing beliefs | Miss disconfirming evidence |
| **Prosecutor** | Attack others' beliefs | Win arguments, lose truth |
| **Politician** | Say what the audience wants to hear | Optimize for approval, not accuracy |
| **Scientist** | Test hypotheses, update based on evidence | Slower, but actually correct over time |

**Confident humility:** The sweet spot. Be confident in your ability to learn, humble about your current conclusions. Strong opinions, loosely held is the cliche -- but the key is actually updating, not just claiming you would.

**Questions that force scientist mode:**
- What would change my mind on this?
- What is the strongest version of the opposing argument?
- When was the last time I changed my mind about something important?
- Am I defending this position because I believe it, or because I said it publicly?

### 6. Operators vs. Talkers (Taleb)

Taleb's heuristic from Skin in the Game for evaluating whose judgment to trust:

**Operators** have made decisions with real consequences. They have skin in the game. Their judgment is calibrated by feedback from reality.

**Talkers** analyze, advise, and comment without bearing consequences. Their judgment is calibrated by what sounds smart, not what works.

| Signal | Operator | Talker |
|--------|----------|--------|
| Track record | Has done the thing, lived with consequences | Has advised, analyzed, or written about the thing |
| Confidence style | Specific about what they know and do not know | Broadly confident about everything |
| Language | Concrete, based on experience | Abstract, based on frameworks |
| When wrong | Loses money, reputation, or time | Publishes a revised analysis |
| Valuable for | Decision-making, execution | Pattern recognition, synthesis |

Both are useful. But in high-stakes decisions, weight operators 3:1 over talkers. The person who has actually built a company in this market knows things the analyst never will.

### 7. Reversible vs. Irreversible Decisions

Not all decisions deserve the same process. Use decision weight to match process to stakes.

| Type | Speed | Process | Example |
|------|-------|---------|---------|
| **Type 1 (irreversible, high-stakes)** | Slow, deliberate | Full analysis, pre-mortem, multiple perspectives | Acquisition, major pivot, key hire |
| **Type 2 (reversible, low-stakes)** | Fast, bias for action | Decide quickly, course-correct | Feature experiment, pricing test, tool choice |

Most organizations make the mistake of treating Type 2 decisions like Type 1. This creates decision paralysis. The cost of a slow Type 2 decision is usually higher than the cost of a wrong one -- because you can reverse it.

Conversely, treating a Type 1 decision like a Type 2 (moving fast on something irreversible) can be catastrophic.

### 8. Prospect Theory Quick Reference (Kahneman and Tversky)

People do not evaluate outcomes rationally. They evaluate them relative to a reference point, and losses loom larger than equivalent gains.

**Key implications for decisions:**
- A guaranteed $900 feels better than a 90% chance of $1,000 (risk aversion for gains)
- A guaranteed loss of $900 feels worse than a 90% chance of losing $1,000 (risk seeking for losses)
- People will take irrational gambles to avoid certain losses
- Framing a decision as "what you lose" vs. "what you gain" changes the outcome

**Practical use:** When presenting options to stakeholders, be aware that framing determines the choice. If you want risk-taking, frame as losses to avoid. If you want conservative choices, frame as gains to protect.

## Application Checklist

- [ ] Classify the decision: Type 1 (irreversible) or Type 2 (reversible)?
- [ ] Match the process to the decision weight
- [ ] Check: is this a situation where System 1 (intuition) is reliable?
- [ ] Run the bias checklist -- which biases are most likely active?
- [ ] For planning: use reference class forecasting, not inside-view estimation
- [ ] Conduct a pre-mortem: what could go wrong?
- [ ] Ask: who am I listening to? Operators or talkers?
- [ ] Check for sunk cost: would I start this today if I had not already invested?
- [ ] State what evidence would change your mind
- [ ] For the final decision: can you articulate the strongest counterargument?

## Anti-Patterns

**Analysis Paralysis** -- Applying Type 1 process to Type 2 decisions. If the decision is reversible and low-stakes, decide in under 10 minutes and move on.

**Gut Feel on Novel Decisions** -- Trusting intuition in situations where you lack deep domain experience. System 1 is only reliable in familiar, regular environments. A first-time founder's "gut feel" about market timing is pattern matching on too small a sample.

**The Smart Person Trap** -- Assuming that because someone is intelligent, their judgment on a specific domain is reliable. Intelligence is domain-general; judgment is domain-specific. A brilliant physicist has no special insight into go-to-market strategy.

**Consensus as Quality Signal** -- Believing a decision is good because everyone agrees. Consensus often means the group converged on the least threatening option, not the best one. Healthy decisions often involve productive disagreement.

**Post-Hoc Rationalization** -- Deciding emotionally, then constructing a logical narrative to justify it. The tell: you cannot articulate what would make you change your mind, because the logic was reverse-engineered from the conclusion.

**Ignoring Base Rates** -- Believing your project is special enough to beat the odds without identifying specifically what makes it different. The base rate for startups, product launches, and large projects is failure. You need a concrete reason to believe yours is different, not just optimism.
