# Competitive Positioning

## When to Use

- Entering a new market or launching a new product
- Evaluating whether your current strategy is actually a strategy versus a wish list
- Choosing between multiple market opportunities
- Responding to a competitive threat or market shift
- Building an investor narrative around defensibility

## Core Frameworks

### 1. The Strategy Kernel (Rumelt)

Every real strategy has three parts. If any is missing, you have aspirations, not strategy.

| Component | What It Answers | Test |
|-----------|----------------|------|
| **Diagnosis** | What is the nature of the challenge? | Can you name the 1-2 critical obstacles in plain language? |
| **Guiding Policy** | What is our overall approach to overcome it? | Does it create focus by ruling things out? |
| **Coherent Actions** | What specific, coordinated steps follow? | Do the actions reinforce each other, or pull in different directions? |

The diagnosis is the part most people skip. They jump from "we need to grow" to a list of initiatives. Rumelt calls this the most important step: naming the challenge correctly is half the work. A hospital system whose diagnosis is "we have a quality problem" will build a different strategy than one whose diagnosis is "we have a coordination problem between departments."

### 2. The Strategic Choice Cascade (Lafley and Martin)

Five choices that must be internally consistent:

```
1. What is our winning aspiration?
      |
2. Where will we play?
   (geographies, segments, channels, categories)
      |
3. How will we win?
   (value proposition, competitive advantage)
      |
4. What capabilities must we have?
      |
5. What management systems are required?
```

The critical discipline: "Where to play" and "How to win" are the two choices that matter most, and they must reinforce each other. Choosing to play in premium skincare but competing on price is incoherent. P&G won in mass-market consumer goods by choosing to win on brand building plus retail relationships plus consumer understanding. Those three capabilities formed a mutually reinforcing system.

**Where to Play dimensions:**
- Customer segments (who specifically)
- Product/service categories (what you offer)
- Geographic markets (where physically)
- Channels (how you reach them)
- Vertical stages (what part of the value chain)

**How to Win options (pick one, be honest):**
- Cost leadership (you are cheaper because of structural advantage)
- Differentiation (you are better in a way customers pay for)
- Niche focus (you serve a narrow segment better than anyone)

### 3. Sources of Competitive Power

Rumelt identifies these sources of advantage. Rate yourself honestly on each:

| Source | Description | Durability |
|--------|-------------|------------|
| **Leverage** | Concentrate force on a pivotal objective | Medium -- works until competitors notice |
| **Proximate objectives** | Achievable goals that create momentum | Short -- but compounds |
| **Chain-link systems** | Every link must be strong; competitors cannot copy one piece | High -- hard to replicate a system |
| **Design** | Tight integration of resources and actions | High -- requires deep understanding |
| **Focus** | Coordination around a single policy | Medium -- requires ongoing discipline |
| **Growth through advantage** | Grow because you have an advantage, not to create one | Depends on underlying advantage |
| **Inertia and entropy** | Exploit competitors' inability to change | Medium -- windows close |

### 4. Bad Strategy Detection

Rumelt's four hallmarks of bad strategy. If you see any of these, stop and rediagnose:

1. **Fluff** -- Restatement of the obvious using inflated language. "We will leverage our synergistic capabilities to deliver customer-centric solutions." This says nothing.
2. **Failure to face the challenge** -- The strategy does not acknowledge what is actually hard. If your strategy works equally well whether the market goes up or down, it is not a strategy.
3. **Mistaking goals for strategy** -- "Our strategy is to grow 20% per year." That is an aspiration. Strategy is the bridge between here and there.
4. **Bad strategic objectives** -- A laundry list of disconnected priorities. If everything is a priority, nothing is.

### 5. Building Defensibility

Defensibility is not just IP or network effects. It is the full system:

**Structural moats:**
- Network effects (each user makes the product better for others)
- Switching costs (pain of leaving exceeds pain of staying)
- Economies of scale (unit costs drop as volume increases)
- Counter-positioning (incumbents cannot copy you without damaging themselves)

**Operational moats:**
- Accumulated learning (you get better faster than competitors)
- Process excellence (chain-link systems where every step matters)
- Culture and talent density (hard to replicate, easy to destroy)

**The contrarian truth test:** Can you state what you believe that most of your market disagrees with? If your strategy is consensus, your returns will be consensus. The best positions are ones where you can articulate specifically why smart people disagree with you, and why they are wrong.

### 6. Where to Play Decision Matrix

Use this to evaluate market opportunities:

| Criterion | Market A | Market B | Market C |
|-----------|----------|----------|----------|
| Size of prize (TAM) | | | |
| Right to win (existing capabilities) | | | |
| Competitive intensity | | | |
| Customer willingness to pay | | | |
| Switching cost potential | | | |
| Speed to defensibility | | | |
| Alignment with guiding policy | | | |

Score 1-5 for each. But do not just add up scores -- the cascade must be coherent. A market you score 5/5 on size but 1/5 on right-to-win is a trap.

### 7. The Activity System Test (Lafley and Martin)

A strategy is only as strong as the system of activities that supports it. Individual activities can be copied; a tightly interlocking system cannot.

**How to build an activity system:**
1. List the 5-8 most important activities your organization performs
2. Draw connections between activities that reinforce each other
3. Test: if a competitor copied any single activity, would it work without the others?
4. The more connections between activities, the harder the system is to replicate

**Example -- IKEA's activity system:**
- Self-serve warehouse format (reduces staff cost)
- Flat-pack design (reduces shipping and storage cost)
- In-house design team (controls aesthetic and modularity)
- Suburban store locations (cheap real estate, large footprint)
- Minimal customer service (trade-off for low price)
- Restaurants in store (extend visit time, increase basket size)

Each activity makes the others more effective. A competitor could copy flat-pack design, but without the warehouse format, suburban locations, and in-house design team, flat-pack alone does not create the same advantage. Rumelt calls this a chain-link system: the whole is only as strong as the weakest link, but a complete chain is nearly impossible to replicate.

**Your activity system audit:**
- List your core activities
- For each pair, ask: does activity A make activity B more effective?
- Count the reinforcing connections
- If fewer than 5 connections exist among your core activities, your strategy lacks coherence
- If a competitor could copy your most impressive activity and get 80% of the benefit in isolation, you do not have a system

## Application Checklist

- [ ] Write a one-paragraph diagnosis of the competitive challenge in plain language
- [ ] Identify what you are choosing NOT to do (strategy is as much about exclusion)
- [ ] State your guiding policy in one sentence
- [ ] List 3-5 coherent actions that reinforce each other
- [ ] Test: do any of Rumelt's four bad strategy hallmarks apply?
- [ ] Define "where to play" across all five dimensions
- [ ] Define "how to win" -- pick one primary mode and commit
- [ ] Identify the 2-3 capabilities required to execute
- [ ] Name the management systems that keep capabilities sharp
- [ ] Articulate your contrarian truth -- what do you believe that the market does not?
- [ ] Identify at least one structural and one operational moat you are building toward
- [ ] Pressure-test: if a competitor read this strategy, could they easily copy it?

## Anti-Patterns

**The Do Everything Strategy** -- Trying to serve every segment, through every channel, with every feature. This is the opposite of strategy. Lafley argues the most important choices are what NOT to do.

**The Aspiration Trap** -- Confusing ambitious goals with strategy. "Be the number one platform in our category" is not a strategy. The question is how, given specific obstacles.

**Copying the Leader** -- Benchmarking competitors and doing what they do, slightly better. This guarantees you are always behind. Positioning requires difference, not just improvement.

**The Capabilities Fantasy** -- Choosing where to play based on market attractiveness alone, without honestly assessing whether you have or can build the right-to-win capabilities.

**Strategy by Committee** -- Letting every stakeholder add their priority to the strategy document until it is a 47-item list. Real strategy makes people uncomfortable because it says no to things they care about.

**Confusing Operational Effectiveness with Strategy** -- Being better at execution is not positioning. If everyone in your industry adopts the same best practices, no one has an advantage. Strategy is about being different, not just being better.
